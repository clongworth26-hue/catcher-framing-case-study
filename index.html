Stolen Strikes:
The One-Knee-Down Catching Revolution

A data-grounded case study of how the low receiving setup took over MLB and became the single most valuable framing weapon of the Statcast era — measured in stolen strikes and the runs they save.

Data: Baseball Savant (Statcast) catcher-framing & catcher-stance leaderboards · Compiled 2026-10-16 · 663 catcher-seasons framing (2015–2025) · 331 catcher-seasons stance splits (2020–2025)

A decade ago almost every catcher received from a crouch — both knees up, a conventional balance between framing, blocking, and throwing. Today, virtually every MLB catcher drops to one knee to receive. The machine-tracked data shows exactly when it happened, and exactly why: dropping a knee buys more called strikes in the shadow zone, and those extra strikes translate directly into runs — the most valuable, lowest-cost run prevention a team can find behind the plate.

1. The adoption curve — a near-universal takeover

Statcast began directly tracking catcher stance in 2020 (a knee counts as "down" below ~9 inches off the ground). The rate of pitches received from a knee-down setup went from roughly one in four to nineteen in twenty in just six seasons.

When did it become popular?

Chad's instinct was right to ask — the knee-down setup was a niche curiosity until ~2019–2021. Pioneering orgs (most famously the Yankees' catching lab, plus the broader Driveline/"Framing+" research wave) proved the upside, and by 2024–2025 it was near-universal (Austin Wells ~97%, Will Smith ~94% knee-down in the 2024 World Series). The Statcast stance data picks up the story exactly as it went mainstream: 23.7% in 2020 to 95.3% in 2025.

3. The money stat — framing runs saved, by stance

Sabr/Savant converts each extra called strike into run value — approximately +0.125 runs per stolen strike. Summed across the league and normalized per 1,000 received pitches, knee-down receiving isn't just a hair better — it's the difference between positive and negative framing value.

League aggregate (2020–2025): across 331 qualified catcher-seasons, knee-down setups produced +244.4 framing runs while traditional setups produced -168.4 runs. The stance isn't a gimmick — it's where essentially all modern framing value now lives.

4. An honest caveat — stance ≠ skill

Comparing a catcher's knee-down rate (how often they use it) against their framing value yields a very weak correlation (r ≈ 0.052, n=331). That's expected and important: dropping a knee does not automatically make a catcher a good framer. The causal driver is receiving hand/glove work and body quietness. Knee-down is the platform that lets already-gifted receivers maximize that skill — low and locked on the low pitch. It's not a magic bullet, which is why teams still teach glove skill first.

6. The bottom line

The one-knee-down stance is the dominant catcher innovation of the TrackMan era because it cleanly converts pitches into called strikes into runs — the same batter-run value at effectively zero resource cost. It took MLB from ~24% knee-down in 2020 to ~95% in 2025, and it's the platform behind essentially every elite framing season since. For any catcher (or program) trying to gain an edge, the lesson is simple: master glove work first, then get low and steal the bottom of the zone.
